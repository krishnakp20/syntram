AI can now handle a meaningful share of routine customer support across chat, email and phone. But many support automation projects disappoint — not because the technology is incapable, but because they skip the groundwork.

This guide covers what a well-run project looks like, step by step.

## Step 1: Understand your demand

Before choosing tools, understand why customers contact you.

- **Export a representative sample** of recent tickets, chats and call notes.
- **Categorise by intent** — order status, password reset, billing question, product how-to, complaint and so on.
- **Note how each is resolved:** what information was needed, which systems were used, and whether a policy decision was involved.

You're looking for intents that are **frequent**, **well-defined**, and **resolvable with information or actions available in your systems**. These are your automation candidates.

<Callout type="note">
Intents that require judgement, negotiation, empathy in difficult situations or exceptions to policy are usually poor first candidates. Plan for those to go to people — with better context.
</Callout>

## Step 2: Choose a first use case

Pick one or two intents to start with. A good first use case:

- Represents a noticeable share of volume
- Has a clear, checkable definition of "resolved"
- Needs data from no more than one or two systems
- Has low risk if something goes wrong

Order status, delivery questions, appointment changes and common how-to questions are typical starting points.

## Step 3: Get your knowledge in order

AI assistants answer from the information you give them. If your help centre is outdated or contradictory, the assistant will be too.

- **Audit the relevant help content** for accuracy and gaps.
- **Write down unwritten rules** that agents know but customers can't see.
- **Assign ownership** — someone must keep this content current.
- **Structure it for retrieval:** clear titles, one topic per article, explicit conditions ("If your order was placed more than 30 days ago…").

Most modern assistants use **retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)**: they search your approved content for each question and answer from what they find, ideally citing it. This is usually more reliable and easier to maintain than training a custom model.

## Step 4: Connect the systems

Information answers questions; integrations resolve them.

- **Read access** to order, account, booking or ticket data lets the assistant give specific answers ("Your order shipped yesterday…").
- **Write access** lets it complete tasks — updating an address, rescheduling, starting a return — within rules you define.
- **Verification** must come first: confirm who the customer is before sharing or changing personal data.

Design every action with limits. For example: refunds may be initiated automatically below a set value and within policy; anything else goes to a person.

## Step 5: Design the handoff

Handoff is where customer trust is won or lost.

- **Make it easy to reach a person**, and never trap customers in loops.
- **Escalate automatically** when the request is out of scope, the customer is frustrated, or the topic is sensitive.
- **Pass full context:** the conversation, customer details, what was attempted and a short summary.
- **Route intelligently** to the right team or skill group.

When agents receive a well-summarised case instead of a cold start, handling becomes faster — even for the conversations AI didn't resolve.

## Step 6: Choose channels deliberately

You don't need every channel on day one.

- **Web chat and messaging** are often the easiest place to start: customers expect quick answers, and replies can include links and structured information.
- **Email** benefits from triage, drafting and summarisation, even when a person sends the final reply.
- **Phone** offers big gains for high call volumes, but requires real-time voice engineering. See our [guide to how AI voice agents work](/guides/how-ai-voice-agents-work).

Wherever possible, share one knowledge base and one set of integrations across channels so answers stay consistent.

## Step 7: Test before launch

- **Build an evaluation set** from real past conversations, with expected outcomes.
- **Include edge cases:** ambiguous questions, angry customers, missing data, attempts to get the assistant off-topic.
- **Score answers** for accuracy, completeness, tone and correct escalation.
- **Have experienced agents review** a sample — they'll spot problems no metric catches.

Re-run the same evaluation whenever you change content, prompts or models.

## Step 8: Roll out gradually

1. **Internal pilot** — your support team uses the assistant first.
2. **Limited exposure** — a percentage of conversations, specific hours, or a single channel.
3. **Close review** of transcripts and handoffs during the first weeks.
4. **Expand** to more traffic and intents once quality is stable.

## Step 9: Measure what matters

Agree success measures before launch. Useful ones include:

- **Resolution rate** for automated conversations — resolved without a person, and confirmed rather than assumed
- **Escalation quality** — were handoffs appropriate, and did they include useful context?
- **Customer satisfaction** for automated vs human-handled conversations
- **Repeat contact rate** — did customers come back about the same issue?
- **Agent time** spent on routine vs complex work

Be wary of a high "containment" figure on its own: a conversation that ended without escalation isn't necessarily a resolved one.

## Common pitfalls

- **Automating before fixing content.** Bad knowledge produces confident wrong answers.
- **No clear owner** once the project goes live.
- **Hiding the human option** to inflate automation figures.
- **Launching everywhere at once** instead of learning from a focused rollout.
- **Ignoring the agent experience** — support teams should help shape and review the system.

## Summary

Successful support automation is less about the model and more about the system around it: accurate knowledge, solid integrations, careful handoff, honest measurement and gradual rollout. Start small, prove value on real conversations and build from there.
