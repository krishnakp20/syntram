An AI voice agent looks simple from the outside: a caller speaks, the agent replies. Underneath, it's a real-time distributed system where a dozen components must cooperate within a fraction of a second, on every turn, for the whole call.

This guide walks through that architecture layer by layer, with the design decisions that matter at each one. It's aimed at technical leads and product owners planning a voice agent project.

## The architecture at a glance

A typical production voice agent has these layers:

1. **Telephony & media** — connects calls and streams audio
2. **Speech recognition** — turns audio into text, continuously
3. **Turn management** — decides when to listen and when to speak
4. **Orchestration & reasoning** — the LLM plus conversation state
5. **Tools & business logic** — the actions the agent can take
6. **Speech synthesis** — turns replies into audio
7. **Handoff** — transfers to people with context
8. **Observability & analytics** — logs, transcripts, metrics and evaluation

Let's look at each.

## 1. Telephony and media

**What it does:** answers or places calls, streams audio in both directions, and handles transfers, holds and recordings.

**Common options:**

- **SIP trunking** into your existing phone system or contact-centre platform
- **Cloud telephony providers** with programmable voice APIs
- **WebRTC** for voice inside websites and mobile apps
- **Real-time media frameworks** that manage audio rooms and streaming between participants

**Key decisions:**

- Keep your existing numbers and contact-centre stack, or route calls through a new provider?
- Where does the agent sit — in front of your human team, alongside it as overflow, or only after hours?
- How will warm transfers work technically — SIP transfer, conference bridge, or a contact-centre API?

## 2. Speech recognition (STT)

**What it does:** converts the caller's speech into text in real time.

**Key decisions:**

- **Streaming vs batch.** Real-time agents need streaming recognition with partial results.
- **Language and accent coverage.** Test with recordings that represent your actual callers.
- **Custom vocabulary.** Names, products, places and codes are where generic recognition fails most often.
- **Numbers and spelling.** Phone numbers, reference codes and email addresses often need dedicated handling — for example, confirming them back to the caller.

## 3. Turn management

**What it does:** decides when the caller has finished, when the agent should speak, and what happens when they talk over each other.

This layer has an outsized effect on how natural the conversation feels:

- **End-of-turn detection** based on silence alone tends to be either too slow or too eager. Content-aware detection performs better.
- **Barge-in** lets the caller interrupt. The agent must stop speaking quickly and discard the rest of its planned reply.
- **Backchannel and holding phrases** ("Sure, let me check…") help during unavoidable waits, such as slow API calls.

## 4. Orchestration and reasoning

**What it does:** keeps track of the conversation and decides what to do next.

At the centre is usually a large language model, wrapped in an orchestration layer that controls it. Two broad patterns exist:

- **Free-form agent:** the LLM follows a single set of instructions and decides everything. Fast to prototype, harder to make consistent.
- **Structured flow:** the conversation is modelled as states or steps (greet → identify → understand → act → confirm), with the LLM handling language understanding and generation inside each step. More work up front, but more predictable and easier to test.

For business-critical calls, we generally recommend a structured approach with the LLM doing what it does best — understanding varied phrasing and producing natural replies — while state, rules and permissions live in code.

**Other decisions at this layer:**

- **Model selection** per task, balancing quality, speed and cost
- **Context management** — what history and knowledge the model sees on each turn
- **Guardrails** — topics the agent must not discuss, information it must not reveal, and when it must escalate

<Callout type="tip" title="Keep business rules out of the prompt">
Eligibility checks, pricing, refund limits and similar rules should be enforced in code that the agent calls, not described in instructions and left to the model's judgement. Prompts guide behaviour; code guarantees it.
</Callout>

## 5. Tools and business logic

**What it does:** gives the agent real capabilities in your systems.

Tools are functions the LLM can request, such as `find_available_slots`, `book_appointment`, `get_order_status` or `create_ticket`. Each tool should:

- Have a **narrow, well-defined purpose** and validated inputs
- **Enforce permissions** — the agent can only act for the verified caller
- Return **concise, structured results** the model can use in a reply
- Be **idempotent** where possible, so a retry doesn't create duplicates
- Have **timeouts and fallbacks**, so a slow system doesn't stall the call

Most of the integration effort in a voice project is here — connecting to CRMs, calendars, order systems and helpdesks reliably.

## 6. Speech synthesis (TTS)

**What it does:** turns the agent's replies into natural-sounding speech.

**Key decisions:**

- **Voice selection** that suits your brand and your callers
- **Streaming output** so playback starts on the first sentence
- **Pronunciation** of brand names, places and abbreviations
- **Language support** if you serve callers in more than one language

## 7. Human handoff

**What it does:** transfers the call to a person when the agent should not or cannot continue.

Design handoff deliberately:

- **Triggers:** caller asks for a person, the request is out of scope, the agent's confidence is low, or the topic is sensitive
- **Context transfer:** the receiving person should get the caller's identity, the transcript or a summary, and what the agent already tried
- **Fallbacks:** if no one is available, take a message, offer a callback or provide clear next steps

A poor handoff wipes out the benefit of everything the agent did before it.

## 8. Observability and analytics

**What it does:** shows what's happening on every call and whether the agent is doing its job.

At a minimum, capture:

- **Transcripts and recordings** (subject to your consent and retention policies)
- **Per-turn latency** by pipeline stage
- **Tool calls**, their inputs, results and errors
- **Call outcomes** — resolved, transferred, abandoned — and reasons

Then use it:

- **Review samples of real calls** regularly, especially failures and transfers
- **Maintain an evaluation set** of representative conversations and re-run it whenever prompts, models or flows change
- **Track trends** in why customers call — often the most valuable insight a voice project produces

## Security and compliance considerations

- **Caller verification** before sharing or changing personal data
- **Data minimisation** — only send models the data they need for the current step
- **Provider settings** that control data retention and model training on your data
- **Redaction** of sensitive information in transcripts and logs
- **Disclosure** that the caller is speaking with an automated agent, and consent for recording where required
- **Outbound calling rules**, which vary by jurisdiction and use case

## Putting it together: a sensible first project

1. Choose one high-volume call type with a clear outcome.
2. Map the conversation and the systems it needs to touch.
3. Build the pipeline end to end with real integrations, even if the flow is small.
4. Test with realistic calls — including difficult ones — and measure latency and completion.
5. Launch to a limited share of calls with close review, then expand.
